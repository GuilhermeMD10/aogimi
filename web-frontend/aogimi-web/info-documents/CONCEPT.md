# Aogimi — the idea

No code, no file paths, no architecture. Just what the app is, why it exists, and
what you can do with it. (The technical companion is the repo-root
[`README.md`](../../../README.md).)

---

## The one-sentence version

**Aogimi turns the Japanese you actually read into the Japanese you actually
know.**

---

## The problem it was built for

Anyone learning Japanese ends up living in two places at once.

In one, you're reading — a novel, a manga, an article — and every few lines
there's a word you don't know. In the other, you're studying flashcards
someone else wrote, about words you've never met in a sentence.

The two never touch. The word you struggled with on page 40 doesn't become a
card unless you stop reading, switch apps, look it up, copy it out, type in the
sentence you found it in, and pick a deck for it. Nobody does that for long. So
the reading stays hard and the studying stays abstract.

Aogimi collapses the two into one place. The book, the dictionary and the deck
are the same app, and moving between them takes a click.

---

## The loop

**Read → look up → keep → study → watch it grow.**

1. **Read.** Bring your own books. Open one and read it in the app, laid out the
   way Japanese should be — including right to left and top to bottom when the
   book asks for it.
2. **Look up.** Tap or select a word you don't know. The dictionary opens right
   beside the page, or floats over it — you never leave the book.
3. **Keep.** One more click turns that entry into a flashcard, and it takes the
   sentence you found it in with it. The card remembers the context, not just
   the word.
4. **Study.** Later, the app hands you those cards back, at the moment you're
   about to forget them.
5. **Watch it grow.** Every card you keep is a star. Your whole vocabulary is a
   night sky you can fly through.

That last step is the point of the whole thing. Progress in language learning is
invisible — the reward for a year of study is that reading is slightly less
painful than it was. Aogimi makes it something you can look at.

## What you can do

### Your library

Import your own EPUBs and PDFs — novels, manga, textbooks, anything. They become
a shelf of covers, with the book you're partway through raised to the top as a
"continue reading" card. Filter by what you're reading, what you haven't started,
and what you've finished; search across the shelf.

**Your files stay yours.** The books themselves never leave your device — nothing
is uploaded to a server. What syncs is the *shape* of your library: which books
you own, and how far you've read.

### Reading

Books open in a reader that gets out of the way: one page, one quiet toolbar.

- **Novels and articles** reflow to your settings — text size, line spacing,
  typeface, page colour (light, dark or sepia), paged or scrolling, and
  horizontal or vertical Japanese.
- **Manga and picture books** get a page-image reader instead: one page, two
  facing pages, or continuous scroll, turning right to left the way they should.
- **PDFs** open too.

The page colour is deliberately its own setting, separate from the app's. What
the app looks like is a matter of taste; what the *paper* looks like is a matter
of reading comfort, and they aren't the same question.

**Your place is always kept.** Close the book, close the tab, close the laptop —
you come back to the line you left. And because your position syncs, you can put
the book down on your desktop and pick it up on your phone at the same page.

### The dictionary

Full JMdict and KANJIDIC2 — the same open dictionaries serious Japanese tools are
built on. Search from anywhere in the app, or from inside a book without leaving
the page.

An entry gives you:

- **Meanings**, grouped by part of speech.
- **Readings**, with **pitch accent drawn as a diagram** — the shape of the pitch
  rising and falling across the word, not a number you have to decode.
- **JLPT level**, so you know roughly where a word sits in difficulty.
- **Every kanji in the word**, broken out with its own readings and meanings.
- **Example sentences.**
- **Conjugation help.** Look up 食べさせられなかった and it tells you what you're
  actually looking at — the dictionary form, and the chain of endings that got
  you there. This is the thing beginners most need and most dictionaries don't do.

Names get their own section. Recent lookups are one click away. Everything is
linkable, so a lookup can be shared or bookmarked.

### Decks and cards

Cards are grouped into decks you name. A card carries the word, its reading, its
meanings, its JLPT level — and **the sentence you met it in**, which is the
difference between memorising a gloss and remembering a word.

You can make a card from a book, from the dictionary, or from the deck screen
itself. Search across every card you own, sort by when you added them or by how
well you know them, edit and delete freely.

### Studying

Spaced repetition, but honest about the fact that people are lazy: instead of
configuring a study plan, you press one button.

Answer each card **Again**, **Hard**, **Good**, or **Easy**. The app tracks two things
about every card — how hard it is for *you*, and how long the memory is lasting —
and schedules the next showing from both. You can undo a mistaken answer.

Cards climb a four-step ladder: **New → Seen → Learned → Mastered.** A promotion
comes from the memory genuinely lasting longer — not from counting answers — so
the level means something. Each card shows how far along it is toward the next
step.

You choose what a session is:

- Everything that's due today.
- Just one deck.
- The hardest cards across all your decks.
- Or one of several other orders — oldest first, newest only, random, in the
  order you added them.

You also choose how much help the prompt gives you, from four presets: show the
reading and the context sentence, show a little, or strip it down to the bare
word. Same cards, different difficulty — the same deck can be easy on a tired
evening and brutal when you want it to be.

### The sky

**The centrepiece.** Your decks page isn't a list — it's a star map.

Every card you own is a star. Every deck is a constellation. Cards added on the
same day cluster together, so the map is also a record of when you were studying
and when you weren't. Zoom out and thousands of cards resolve into soft
nebulae — you see the *shape* of your knowledge. Fly into a deck and the
individual stars come back.

A star's **rank is visible in its shape, not just its colour**: new words are bare
points, and a mastered word is a four-pointed sparkle. You can read your progress
across the room, or colourblind.

The map is **yours specifically.** Every account gets its own permanent seed, so
your sky's arrangement is unique and never rearranges itself. Add a card today
and it lands in a spot it will keep forever.

Underneath, a ledger keeps the honest numbers: days studied, stars in your sky,
due today, mastered — and a feed of the cards that just moved up a rank.

Four sky palettes to choose from — Aogimi, Ginga silver, Ember dusk, Aurora —
which recolours both the stars *and* every progress indicator in the app, so a
pink star and its pink progress bar always mean the same thing.

### Your account

A quiet profile page: who you are, what you've read, and a short list of
settings — the theme, your sky's colours, help, credits, and the button that
deletes everything if you want it gone.

Everything that should follow you follows you: decks, cards, review history,
reading positions, study preferences, which devices hold which books. Everything
that shouldn't leave your machine doesn't: the book files themselves.

## The principles behind it

**Reading comes first.** Every feature exists to keep you reading. The dictionary
opens beside the page instead of replacing it. Making a card is one click, not a
form. Nothing asks you to stop.

**Your books are yours.** Bring your own files. They stay on your device. There
is no store, no catalogue, no DRM, nothing to subscribe to for access to your own
library.

**Progress should be visible.** Language learning gives almost no feedback for
months at a time. The sky exists to give it back — not as a number that goes up,
but as something that looks like something.

**Context over vocabulary lists.** A word remembered from the sentence you met it
in survives; a word memorised off a list doesn't. Every card carries where it
came from.

**Calm, not gamified.** No streaks nagging you, no confetti, no owl. The night
sky, quiet type, one accent colour used twice. The reward for studying is
watching your sky fill up, and that's enough.

**Nothing that isn't real.** No fake statistics, no motivational estimates, no
"you're 73% fluent!". If a number is on screen, it's counted from something you
actually did.

**One app, three devices.** Desktop and laptop in the browser; iPhone and Android
in the native app. Put a book down on one, pick it up on another.

## Where it is right now

Aogimi is a personal project under active development, not a shipped product.

What works today: importing and reading your own books, the full dictionary,
decks and cards, the spaced-repetition runner, the star map, cross-device sync of
everything that should sync.

What's still coming: a light theme (the app is currently pinned to its Midnight
look while the frosted-glass design is being finished), the mobile app reaching
parity, and open sign-ups — account creation is closed while the app is being
built.
